The problem

The internet is no longer the public square it was. This isn't a feeling — it's the record.

01

Internet freedom has fallen for 15 straight years

In 2025 it got worse in 27 countries and better in only 17. The report covers 87% of the world's internet users.

Freedom House, 2025 (D1)

02

More than half of humanity lives without a free press

4.25 billion people in 42 "very serious" countries — for the first time, the global average is "difficult".

RSF, 2025 Index (D3)

03

Internet shutdowns are now a tool of government

Iran disconnected the internet in June 2025; Russia blocks websites by allow-listing; China blocks even Tor's bridges.

Tor Project / HRW, 2025 (D12)

04

Your ISP gave you a house with no doorbell

Major carriers put customers behind CGNAT: you can't open a single port. Publishing from home is impossible the classic way.

T-Mobile, Verizon, AT&T (D8)

05

Publishing costs money — or it costs your data

A rented server runs €4–6 a month, forever. "Free" hosting is paid for with your information and your audience in someone else's hands.

06

Everything you upload passes through a third party

Every photo, document or message you share lives on another company's computer, under another country's laws, readable by people you don't know.

01

It's not paranoia — It's documented

Humans were listening inside your home. Literally. Past tense, documented and admitted by the companies themselves.

Apple — Siri

Contractors heard "confidential medical information, drug deals, and recordings of couples". Apple suspended it after it broke — and in 2025 paid $95M to settle the class action.

The Guardian, 2019 · BBC, 2025 (D16)

Amazon — Alexa

Thousands of employees transcribed conversations: about 1,000 clips per shift, tagged with your name and account number.

CNBC / Bloomberg, 2019 (D17)

Google — Google Home

A contractor leaked over 1,000 recordings; 153 had been captured by accident, with no one saying "OK Google".

VRT NWS, 2019 (D17)

And the breaches

Google+ exposed data from up to 500,000 accounts and knew months before saying so. Amazon was fined €746M for processing data for ads without legal basis — upheld on appeal in 2025.

Google, 2018 · CNPD Luxembourg (D15)

The people who know best unplug the speakers

Switching off voice assistants during sensitive meetings is official policy in the UK government, the Irish parliament, an Indian ministry, and at law firms like Mishcon de Reya.

Why? Because the fear has a technical basis: smart speakers activate by mistake 1.5 to 19 times a day (PETS 2020 study); the CIA and MI5 built a tool that turns TVs into microphones (Weeping Angel, 2017); and Samsung's own privacy policy once warned customers not to discuss sensitive matters in front of their TV (2015).

Sources: UK/India governments, Irish parliament, Bloomberg, The Guardian, The Verge, Mon(IoT)r Lab (D23)

02

Your data has no border

The law that does reach you. "Server in Europe" protects nothing when the company is American: a safe in Madrid whose key is kept by a notary in Delaware.

The CLOUD Act (US, 2018)

Forces any company under US jurisdiction to hand over user data "regardless of where it is stored" — including data about non-Americans. This is their own law, not our interpretation.

18 U.S.C. §2713 · DOJ official FAQ (D13)

Europe's courts already said it

In 2020 the CJEU struck down the Privacy Shield (Schrems II): US surveillance doesn't provide protection equivalent to GDPR.

Court of Justice of the EU (D14)

And it has a price tag

Meta paid €1.2 billion — the largest GDPR fine in history — for continuing to send European data to the US.

Irish Data Protection Commission, 2023 (D14)

An example close to home: TaxDown

The Spanish tax-filing app runs on Amazon Web Services and its own privacy policy says "data may be transferred to the United States". Your tax data — pulled from the tax agency itself — under CLOUD Act reach. And if the US demands it, the law allows barring the company from telling you (gag orders, §2705(b)).

taxdown.es · help.taxdown.es · Cornell LII (D18)

TaxDown does nothing illegal. The problem is that whoever decides whether to hand it over is Amazon. Not TaxDown. Not you.

Condemned by UN rapporteurs and Human Rights Watch for curbing expression and assembly.

Over 75% of the audience, in few hands

Interlude — Madrid's "plutonium briefcase"

It really happened in Madrid — twice: a stolen radioactive case abandoned next to a school in Leganés (2008) and on a street in Usera (2022). It wasn't plutonium: it was a low-category construction gauge. But the story people tell says "plutonium by a school" — and it rings true because Madrid has a real hidden nuclear past.

That's post-truth: a real event with the fear turned up. Which is why every claim in this presentation carries its source.

Real case — Publishing without asking permission

Same mechanism, two urgencies: learning and informing.

The mechanism, plainly

Any app running on your PC — a project on port 3000, a static site — goes onto the Tor network as an .onion address with one command. It works even if your ISP won't let you open ports (CGNAT): .onion only uses outbound connections.

D8

For learners

7 million students use GitHub Education because real-world tools "can be cost-prohibitive". Here the cost is zero — and the Git is yours: enola deploys your own Forgejo, a private GitHub that doesn't depend on GitHub.

education.github.com (D10)

For writers

WordPress — the software that already runs 40% of all websites in the world — is one of the platforms enola publishes with one command. Blog, magazine or full site.

W3Techs, Oct 2026 (D5)

For those who inform

An .onion server has no public IP: there's no address to send a takedown order to, no hosting company to pressure. That's why SecureDrop — the open whistleblower submission system already used by over 70 news organizations worldwide — runs over the Tor network: "sources and journalists connect to SecureDrop using the Tor network", and its design "attempts to completely eliminate third parties from the equation". And why RSF recommends OnionShare to journalists under authoritarian regimes.

Freedom of the Press Foundation, 2021 · docs.securedrop.org (D6) · RSF (D7)

The door with a doorman

You can require an access key — client authorization — and your service becomes invisible to anyone without one: it won't even connect.

The context that makes it urgent: 67 journalists killed and 503 detained in twelve months.

RSF, 2025 (D2)

Honest small print: visitors need Tor Browser.

Real case — Sharing only with the people you choose

The folder only your people see

files publishes a folder from your PC as an .onion with autoindex — a browsable web listing — and a per-person key. Only invited guests connect; the invitation is revocable at any time. Versus Drive/Dropbox/WeTransfer: no copy on someone else's servers, no metadata in third-party hands — the same principle behind SecureDrop "eliminating third parties from the equation".

10

What it doesn't do

The fine print, in large type. This section earns credibility for all the others.

It's Tor: it's slow by design

Data takes six encrypted hops. Perfect for websites, blogs and files; not for heavy streaming or reflex gaming — that's what the built-in VPN is for.

TCP only

Real-time games running on UDP can't ride Tor. Turn-based or relaxed co-op → .onion; live competition → VPN.

Your visitors need Tor Browser

It's a free two-click app, but a real friction: this is not the tool for mass audiences or SEO.

Where Tor is blocked, this isn't enough

In China, Iran or Russia you need special bridges — and sometimes not even those work. A tool is no substitute for common sense or legal protection.

D12

It doesn't promise absolute anonymity

It promises privacy and censorship resistance by design; full safety also depends on your habits.

It's your PC

Switch it off and your corner of the internet sleeps with you. No datacenter keeps it standing for you.
